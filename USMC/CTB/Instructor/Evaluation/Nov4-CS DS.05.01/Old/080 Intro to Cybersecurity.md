## 1. Introduction

In this course we will discover how you can keep yourself, your Marines, and the mission safe with a better understanding of Cyber Security and its impact.

### What is Cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the practice of **protecting data, devices, networks, and systems** from being accessed, damaged, or misused by unauthorized people or software.

Think of it as a blend of:

- **Locks and alarms** (prevention)
- **Surveillance** (monitoring)
- **Emergency plans** (response & recovery)

## 2. Three-Tier Approach

Cyber Security has a three-tier approach when it comes to Risk Management. The three tiers are ***identifying, monitoring, and limiting*** risks to a manageable level. The Primary Goal of risk management is to reduce risk to a level that the organization will accept.

> *“Identify risks, monitor them closely, and limit them to a level the mission can safely accept.”*

### 1. Identifying Risks

👉 *“What could hurt us?”*

- You figure out what assets matter (data, systems, users).
- Then, list possible threats: hackers, insider threats, malware, human error, natural disasters.
- Also identify vulnerabilities: outdated software, weak passwords, lack of backups.  
⚡ Think of it like doing reconnaissance before a battle—you can’t defend if you don’t know where you’re weak.

### 2. Monitoring Risks

👉 *“Is it happening right now?”*

- Continuous watch with tools like intrusion detection systems (IDS), Security Information and Event Management (SIEM) logs, and endpoint monitoring.
- Spot trends: Are phishing emails spiking? Is unusual traffic hitting your servers (such as unknown IP addresses or strange requests)?
- Regular risk assessments and audits update your picture of what’s changing.  
⚡ This is like having guards on the walls and scouts outside—watching for attacks in real time.

### 3. Limiting Risks (Mitigation)

👉 *“How do we keep damage under control?”*

- Apply controls: firewalls, encryption, access restrictions, multi-factor authentication.
- Create backups and recovery plans (so if ransomware hits, you can restore quickly).
- Train users (since human error is often the weakest link).  
⚡ Here, you don’t aim for *zero risk* (impossible), but for a level your organization accepts—like putting armor where it matters most.

The goal is **risk reduction to an acceptable level** — not elimination.

- Organizations accept *some* risk (like leaving a window open for ventilation) as long as it doesn’t cripple operations.
- It’s a balancing act between **security** and **business efficiency**.

## 4. Risk Response Techniques

Like Operational Risk Management (I AM IS), organizations use **Risk Response Techniques** to handle cybersecurity risks. The four main methods are:

- **Avoid** – Don’t engage in activities that create risk.
- **Transfer** – Shift the risk to another entity (e.g., insurance or a contractor).
- **Mitigate** – Apply controls to reduce risk impact or likelihood.
- **Accept** – Acknowledge a manageable risk when the cost of controls is higher than the potential impact.

These techniques help leadership make informed decisions to **protect mission-critical systems while balancing cost, effort, and operational needs**.

#### Avoid

An organization can avoid a risk by not providing a service or not participating in a risky activity.  
**Example:** A Marine Corps unit decides **not to connect a classified system to the internet** at all, avoiding exposure to external cyber threats.

#### Transfer

The organization transfers the risk to another entity or at least shares the risk with another entity.  
**Example:** The Corps contracts a **DoD-accredited cloud service provider** to host certain non-critical apps. The provider assumes responsibility for many cyber risks. (Does this sound familiar?)

#### Mitigate

The organization implements controls to reduce risk.  
**Example:** Marines implement **two-person integrity (TPI)** on critical network changes, require **CAC authentication**, and use **intrusion detection systems (IDS)** to reduce risk of unauthorized access.

#### Accept

When the cost of a control outweighs a risk, an organization will often accept the risk.  
**Example:** A forward-deployed unit knows its satellite link has occasional latency vulnerabilities. Since the impact is minor and mission-critical comms are redundantly secured, leadership accepts the risk.
